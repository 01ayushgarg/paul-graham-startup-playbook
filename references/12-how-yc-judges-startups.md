# 12 · How he judges a startup

What PG has written about how YC, and he, decide. The main sources are *Black Swan Farming* [swan, Sep
2012], *What We Look for in Founders* [founders, Oct 2010] and *How to Convince Investors* [convince, Aug
2013].

## Two rules of startup investing [swan, Sep 2012]

He says the returns are concentrated in a few big winners, and "the best ideas look initially like bad
ideas." [swan] As of 2012, he wrote that two companies, Dropbox and Airbnb, accounted for about three
quarters of YC's returns. [swan] So the question to ask isn't whether a startup will succeed but whether it
could succeed really big: "You have to ignore the elephant in front of you, the likelihood they'll succeed."
[swan] He even treats too high a success rate as a warning that YC is being too conservative. [swan]

## Founders over ideas

When YC funded Airbnb, "we thought it was too crazy... We funded them because we liked the founders so
much." [founders] Most founders change their idea [notnot, Mar 2007], so he weighs the people more. In 2005
he put it as "Good people can fix bad ideas, but good ideas can't save bad people." [start, Mar 2005] In 2009
he noted that the YC application asks more about the founders' commitment and relationship than their
ability. [really, Oct 2009]

What investors need, in his 2013 summary: "formidable founders, a promising market, and (usually) some
evidence of success so far." [convince]

## What he'd rather see than credentials

In recent posts he argues that users don't care what's on your resume
[X 2026-03-18](https://x.com/paulg/status/2034324722448466116), and that emphasising credentials to investors
is a red flag [X 2026-08-03](https://x.com/paulg/status/2084094594766327890). The essay version is older: in
*How to Present to Investors* he says not to try to seem more than you are, and to tell stories about
users. [investors, Aug 2006, rev. Sep 2010] In a 2022 post he says the successful founders are the ones
whose early ideas are concrete rather than grand and vague.
[X 2022-03-10](https://x.com/paulg/status/1501821950997508096)

## How to apply: a self-scoring sheet (our suggestion)

The scoring is ours. Each line is anchored to the question it tests. Score 0 (no evidence), 1 (some) or 2
(strong, with a specific example).

| # | Test | Evidence to bring | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Users want it now | Named users, usage, retention | [startupideas] |
| 2 | You'd use it yourself | Your own use, or why you're the exception | [startupideas] |
| 3 | Growth, as a rate | Weekly growth on revenue or active users | [growth] |
| 4 | Default alive | The arithmetic from template 02 | [aord] |
| 5 | Formidable founders | A story of hacking something to your advantage | [founders, convince] |
| 6 | Could be huge | The path from the first users to a big market | [ds, convince] |
| 7 | Why now | What changed recently | [convince] |
| 8 | Two-sentence clarity | The description a friend understands | [startuplessons] |
| 9 | Learned from users | One thing users taught you that changed the product | [users] |

Our reading of how to use the total: a low score on 1, 3 or 9 is the conversation to have first, whatever
the total. A high score on 6 with low scores elsewhere is the "black swan" case [swan]: possible, but bring
the evidence for why.

## Failure modes

- **Pitching likelihood, not size.** He says the size question matters more. [swan]
- **Leading with credentials.** [investors, X 2026-08-03]
- **Grand and vague.** [X 2022-03-10]
- **Treating rules as laws.** He says "there are exceptions to practically every rule."
  [X 2026-09-29](https://x.com/paulg/status/2104956189008150584)

The skill uses these rules as defaults, not laws. When a founder may be an exception, it says so and asks
for the evidence.
