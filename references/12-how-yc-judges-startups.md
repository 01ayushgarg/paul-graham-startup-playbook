# 12 · How he judges a startup

What PG has written about how YC, and he, decide.

## The two rules of startup investing [swan, Sep 2012]

> "Effectively all the returns are concentrated in a few big winners, and... the best ideas look initially
> like bad ideas."

> "Just two companies, Dropbox and Airbnb, account for about three quarters of it." `[2012]`

> "You have to ignore the elephant in front of you, the likelihood they'll succeed, and focus instead on the
> separate and almost invisibly intangible question of whether they'll succeed really big."

> "If we ever got to the point where 100% of the startups we funded were able to raise money after Demo Day,
> it would almost certainly mean we were being too conservative."

## Founders over ideas

> "In fact, when we funded Airbnb, we thought it was too crazy... We funded them because we liked the
> founders so much." [founders, Oct 2010]

> "If you look at the YC application, there are more questions about the commitment and relationship of the
> founders than their ability." [really, Oct 2009]

## What he'd rather see than credentials

> "All that matters in a startup is whether users like the product, and users don't care (either way) what's
> on your resume." [X 2026-03-18](https://x.com/paulg/status/2034324722448466116)

> "It's a red flag to emphasize (or worse still exaggerate) your credentials when talking to investors."
> [X 2026-08-03](https://x.com/paulg/status/2084094594766327890)

> "Early on, the ideas of startup founders can either be grand and vague, or smaller and more concrete...
> the successful founders are the ones whose ideas are concrete." [X 2022-03-10](https://x.com/paulg/status/1501821950997508096)

## Exceptions exist

> "One of the most interesting things about startups is that there are exceptions to practically every
> rule." [X 2026-09-29](https://x.com/paulg/status/2104956189008150584)

The skill uses these rules as defaults, not laws. When a founder may be an exception, it says so and asks
for the evidence.
