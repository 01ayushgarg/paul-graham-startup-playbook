# 11 · Writing and explaining

Founders write all the time: the application, the pitch, the website, the investor update, the one-line
description. PG's position is that this writing is thinking, so unclear writing usually means unclear
thinking.

## Writing is thinking

In *Writes and Write-Nots* he predicts that AI will remove most of the pressure to write, leaving a world
divided into "writes and write-nots". [writes, Oct 2024] His objection is that "writing is thinking. In fact
there's a kind of thinking that can only be done by writing." [writes] He made the same point in a 2023
post: if AI saves people from writing, it also saves them from the ideas writing produces.
[X 2023-01-05](https://x.com/paulg/status/1610812670290325504)

## Write like you talk [talk, Oct 2015]

His trick for getting read is to "write in spoken language." [talk] The test sentence: "Is this the way I'd
say this if I were talking to a friend?" [talk] And a method for any draft: explain it to a friend, then
replace the draft with what you said. [talk] "You don't need complex sentences to express complex ideas."
[talk]

## Write simply [simply, Mar 2021]

Simple writing saves the reader's energy for your ideas. [simply] It also checks you: "If you say nothing
simply, it will be obvious to everyone, including you." [simply] His own process is a fast first draft and
days of editing, much of it cutting. [simply]

## The startup version

> "A startup should be able to explain in one or two sentences exactly what it does." [startuplessons, Apr
> 2006]

Investors use the same test, he says, because if you can't explain your plans concisely, you probably don't
understand them. [convince, Aug 2013] In *How to Present to Investors* the first rule is "Explain what
you're doing", and he adds: "Better a narrow description than a vague one." [investors, Aug 2006, rev. Sep
2010] On standards, his 2026 advice is that the main thing in writing clearly is to have high standards for
clarity. [X 2026-06-02](https://x.com/paulg/status/2061842652765761682)

## How to apply (our suggestion)

A ten-minute pass for any founder document. The steps are ours, built on his three tests.

1. **The two-sentence test.** Write what the company does in at most two sentences, with no adjectives that
   could apply to any startup. [startuplessons, investors]
2. **The friend test.** Read each paragraph and ask his question: is this how I'd say it to a friend? Where
   it isn't, say it aloud and write down what you said. [talk]
3. **The nothing test.** For each sentence, ask what idea it carries. If the honest answer is "none", it was
   hiding behind complexity. Cut it. [simply]
4. **The narrow test.** Replace one vague, broad claim with a narrow, specific one. [investors]
5. **The thinking test.** If a section is hard to write, the problem is usually in the idea. Go back to the
   users, numbers or plan before polishing words. [writes]

## Worked example (invented)

Before: *We are building an AI-powered platform that empowers modern teams to unlock the full potential of
their knowledge.*

After the two-sentence and narrow tests: *We answer engineers' questions about their own codebase in Slack.
Forty teams use it daily; the median team asks 30 questions a week.*

Our reading: the second version is narrower, and it has more information. It also shows the founders know
who the user is.

## Failure modes

- **Impressive over clear:** complex sentences to sound smart. [talk]
- **Boilerplate descriptions:** in his presentation essay, investors discard vague opening sentences as
  meaningless. [investors]
- **Outsourcing the thinking:** letting AI write the pitch, and so never doing the thinking it needed.
  [writes]
- **Over-applying the style:** our reading is that these rules are for explaining. Legal and technical
  documents have other jobs.

**Checks to run on any founder writing:**
1. Can you say what the company does in one or two sentences a friend would understand?
2. Read it aloud. Where does it sound unlike you talking?
3. Which sentences could be cut without losing an idea? Cut them.
4. Which claim is vague, and what's the narrow version?
