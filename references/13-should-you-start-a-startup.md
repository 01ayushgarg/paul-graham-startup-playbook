# 13 · Should you start a startup?

Before ideas, growth or fundraising, there's the prior question. PG has written about it for almost twenty
years: to students in 2005 [start, Mar 2005], to would-be founders weighing a job in 2007 [notnot, Mar
2007], to 14 and 15 year olds in 2024 [google, Mar 2024], and as a warning about what it costs [before, Oct
2014].

## The three ingredients

His 2005 version: "You need three things to create a successful startup: to start with good people, to make
something customers actually want, and to spend as little money as possible." [start] Of the three, people
come first: "Good people can fix bad ideas, but good ideas can't save bad people." [start]

His 2024 version, for people who might start one later, is a different three: "You need to be good at some
kind of technology, you need an idea for what you're going to build, and you need cofounders to start the
company with." [google] And all three come from the same habit: "work on your own projects." [google]
Projects teach you the technology, they are where ideas show up, and "You find cofounders by working on
projects with them." [google]

**Our reading:** the 2005 list is about running a startup and the 2024 list is about being ready to start
one. If you have neither the skill, the idea nor the people yet, the next step isn't a company. It's a
project.

## Sort your reasons, don't just count them

> "Most people who are reluctant to do something have about eight different reasons mixed together in their
> heads, and don't know themselves which are biggest." [notnot]

In *Why to Not Not Start a Startup* he goes through the common reasons one by one and argues that most,
but not all, should be ignored [notnot]. In our words, his verdicts:

| Reason | His verdict, paraphrased [notnot] |
|---|---|
| Too young | Mostly bogus. The real test is whether you act like an adult, not your age. |
| Too inexperienced | Bogus: "if you're too inexperienced to start a startup, what you should do is start one." [notnot] |
| Not determined enough | Often bogus; he thinks most hackers "underestimate their determination." [notnot] |
| Not smart enough | Usually bogus: "If you're smart enough to worry that you might not be smart enough to start a startup, you probably are." [notnot] |
| Know nothing about business | Bogus: early on all you need to know is how to build things people want. |
| No cofounder | Real: "Not having a cofounder is a real problem." [notnot] |
| No idea | Weak, because most startups change their idea anyway. |
| No room for more startups | Bogus: there's no fixed number of startups any more than of jobs. |
| Family to support | Real; he says so plainly and won't advise it. [notnot] |
| Independently wealthy | Real for him; it was his own excuse. |
| Not ready for commitment | Real: a startup takes years, so don't start one if you can't commit to that. |
| Need for structure | Real, if it's genuinely true of you: then you probably shouldn't start one. |
| Fear of uncertainty | Mostly bogus: it will probably fail, and in his experience no one blames you if you made a serious effort. |
| Don't realize what you're avoiding | A real gap in new graduates: people who've worked a year or two know how much jobs suck. |
| Parents want a safe career | Understandable, but parents share more of the downside than the upside. |
| A job is the default | The strongest force of all: "Defaults are enormously powerful, precisely because they operate without any conscious choice." [notnot] |

He is equally plain about the odds: "if you start a startup, it will probably fail." [notnot] And the
case for trying anyway is that "even failure will get you to the ultimate goal faster than getting a job."
[notnot] In 2005 he made the age point directly: failing with a family to support "could be serious. But if
you fail at 22, so what?" [hiring, May 2005]

## The cost side

The other half of the decision is what it takes. "If you start a startup, it will take over your life to a
degree you cannot imagine." [before] He tells students that "Most people should still be searching
breadth-first at 20" [before], and in 2005 he argued that a startup is a way to be valued directly by the
market: "The market is a lot more discerning than any employer." [hiring]

## How to apply (our suggestion)

This is our procedure, built on his list. The steps and the scoring are ours.

1. **Write down every reason you haven't started.** All of them, including the embarrassing ones. Aim for
   his estimate of about eight. [notnot]
2. **Label each one real or bogus** using the table above. Be honest: a family to support and having
   no cofounder are real in his view; being too inexperienced is not. [notnot]
3. **For each real reason, write what would remove it** and when: a cofounder you could build a project
   with this term, savings that cover a year, a date after which the commitment is possible.
4. **For each bogus reason, write the test that would prove it bogus.** "Not smart enough" is tested by
   building something technically hard on a weekend, not by thinking about it.
5. **Check the three ingredients** [google]. Can you build? Is there something your friends actually want?
   Who have you already worked well with?
6. **Decide on one of three answers:** start now; start a project now and decide in six months; or not yet,
   with the real reason named and a date to revisit.

## Worked scenario (invented)

Maya is 24, a backend engineer two years out of college, with $18k saved and no dependants. Her list:

| Reason | Real or bogus | What changes it |
|---|---|---|
| *I don't have an idea* | Bogus, mostly [notnot] | Notice problems at work for a month [startupideas] |
| *I don't have a cofounder* | Real [notnot] | Build a side project with the colleague she trusts most [google] |
| *I'd be giving up a good salary* | The default talking [notnot] | Name it as the default, not a reason |
| *I might fail* | True, and survivable at 24 [hiring] | Nothing to fix |
| *I don't know enough about business* | Bogus [notnot] | Focus on the product; pick up the rest on the fly [notnot] |

One real reason (no cofounder) and four that dissolve. **Our reading of his advice:** her answer is a
project now, with the colleague, and a decision date in six months. If the project gets users, she has all
three ingredients.

## Failure modes

- **Counting reasons instead of weighing them.** Eight bogus reasons are still zero real ones. [notnot]
- **Treating "no idea" as a blocker.** Most startups change their idea anyway. [notnot] See chapter 01.
- **Starting alone because no one is ready.** He calls this a real problem, not a detail. [notnot]
- **Playing house.** Doing the outward things a startup does (incorporating, raising, hiring) before anyone
  wants the product. [before] See chapter 02.
- **Starting because it's fashionable.** Our reading: the costs in [before] are only worth paying if you want
  to build the thing.

## Limits

- *Why to Not Not Start a Startup* was written in 2007 for an audience of mostly young, unattached
  programmers, and its points about YC's application are about YC's process at that time. [notnot]
- He declines to advise anyone with a family to start a startup, rather than saying they shouldn't. [notnot]
- The age points are about students and people in their twenties; they don't say older founders do worse.

**Checks to run:**
1. What are your reasons, and which are real?
2. Of the three ingredients (technology, idea, cofounders), which do you have today? [google]
3. Is the job the default, or a choice? [notnot]
4. Could you commit for several years if it works? [notnot]
