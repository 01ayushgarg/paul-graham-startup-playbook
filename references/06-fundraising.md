# 06 · Fundraising

> "Raising money is the second hardest part of starting a startup. The hardest part is making something
> people want." [fundraising, Aug 2008]

## Fundraising in one sentence

> "Avoid investors till you decide to raise money, and then when you do, talk to them all in parallel,
> prioritized by expected value, and accept offers greedily. That's fundraising in one sentence."
> [fr, Sep 2013]

## The rules [fr]

| Rule | In his words, or paraphrased |
|---|---|
| When | "Don't raise money unless you want it and it wants you." |
| Mode | "A startup should either be in fundraising mode or not." |
| No till yes | Treat investors as saying no until there's a definite offer with no contingencies. |
| Order | "Expected value = how likely an investor is to say yes, multiplied by how good it would be if they did." |
| Next steps | Never leave a meeting without asking what happens next. |
| Close | "It's not a deal till the money's in the bank." |
| *We don't lead* | His translation: no, unless you turn out to be a hot deal. |
| How much | Headcount × $15k × 18 months, as an upper bound (2013 figures). |
| Valuation | At best third, after getting the money and getting good investors. |
| Accept | "If someone makes you an acceptable offer, take it." |
| Dilution | Sell under 15% in the first phase and no more than 25% on top in the second (his 2013 guide). |
| Stop | "Stop fundraising when you start to get a lot of air in the straw." |

`[2013]` The dollar figures and percentages are his 2013 numbers.

## Survival rules [fundraising, Aug 2008]

- Assume any deal will fall through until it closes.
- "Put the startup first." Fit investor meetings around development, not the reverse.
- If someone reputable offers reasonable terms, take it.
- "There is nothing investors like more than a startup that seems like it's going to succeed even without
  them."
- The worst outcome is the long no, after months of meetings.

## How to convince investors [convince, Aug 2013]

> "You need three things: formidable founders, a promising market, and (usually) some evidence of success so
> far."

"Formidable is roughly justifiably confident", and for an inexperienced founder the way to seem formidable
is "to stick to the truth." [convince] Investors think of startups as markets, not ideas, so you need a why
now and "some plausible sequence of hops that leads to dominating a big market." [convince] His summary:
"Make the truth good, then just tell it." [convince]

## How to present [investors, Aug 2006, rev. Apr 2007 and Sep 2010]

Written for YC's Demo Day, where each startup had minutes, not an hour. The two goals: "(a) explain what
you're doing, and (b) explain why users will want it." [investors] His fourteen points, by their headings:

1. Explain what you're doing. 2. Get rapidly to demo. 3. Better a narrow description than a vague one.
4. Don't talk and drive. 5. Don't talk about secondary matters at length. 6. Don't get too deeply into
business models. 7. Talk slowly and clearly at the audience. 8. Have one person talk. 9. Seem confident.
10. Don't try to seem more than you are. 11. Don't put too many words on slides. 12. Specific numbers are
good. 13. Tell stories about users. 14. Make a soundbite stick in their heads. [investors]

His own update: the demo section is "now obsolete" for Demo Day, where talks have become too short to
include much demo, and he thinks he was wrong to stress demos so much. [investors] On competitors: list
the top three and say in one sentence each what they lack that you have. [investors]

Use `templates/05-pitch.md` to draft one.

## Herd dynamics [herd, Aug 2013]

> "The biggest component in most investors' opinion of you is the opinion of other investors."

So the first commitment is the hardest; the second is easier. [herd] And keep investors' names to
yourself: "Do not, however, tell A who B is." [herd]

## Keep control of the board [control, Dec 2010]

In 2010 he reported that, by asking YC founders, he found that "In a dozen companies we've funded, the
founders still had a majority of the board seats after the series A round." [control] The traditional series
A board gave founders two of five seats. [control] He's careful about what control means: investors usually
keep vetoes on big decisions, and "Matters are decided in the discussion preceding the vote, not in the vote
itself." But "while board control is not total control, it's not imaginary either." [control]

**Our reading:** it's a fair thing to ask for if you're a strong company, and it's worth knowing that it was
already common among YC companies in 2010. Norms will have moved since; check current terms.

## Is the trade worth it? The equity equation [equity, Jul 2007]

> "You should give up n% of your company if what you trade it for improves your average outcome enough that
> the (100 - n)% you have left is worth more than the whole company was before."

Break-even multiple = 1 / (1 − n). His example: giving up 7% is worth it if it improves the outcome by more
than about 7.5%. "You should always feel richer after trading equity." [equity]

Worked (ours): selling 20% needs the money to improve your expected outcome by 1 / 0.8 = 1.25x, or 25%.
Selling 25% needs 33%.

## Don't talk to corp dev [corpdev, Jan 2015]

> "Do you want to sell your company right now? Not eventually, right now. If not, just don't take the first
> meeting."

The script: "Sorry, but we're focusing on growing the company." [corpdev] Behind it is his older point that
many acquisitions are really hiring: "When a startup gets bought for 2 or 3 million six months in, it's
really more of a hiring bonus than an acquisition." [hiring, May 2005]

## How to apply (our suggestion)

1. **Decide whether to raise at all.** Are you default alive without it (chapter 04)? Do you want it, and
   does it want you? [fr]
2. **Write the three things** in three true sentences: founders, market, evidence. [convince]
3. **Draft the pitch** with `templates/05-pitch.md`.
4. **Rank investors by expected value** and talk to them in parallel. [fr]
5. **Set a stopping rule** before you start: the amount, a dilution ceiling, and a date. Ours, using his
   2013 guide on dilution. [fr]
6. **Close, then stop.** Nothing counts until the money is in the bank. [fr]

## Failure modes

- **Raising instead of growing.** Fundraising becomes the top idea in your mind (chapter 07). [top]
- **Serial meetings.** He says to talk to investors in parallel [fr]; our reading is that one at a time is
  how you end up with what he calls the worst case, the long no after months of meetings. [fundraising]
- **Optimising valuation first.** [fr]
- **Talking about secondary matters.** [investors]

**Checks to run:**
1. Do you want money, and does it want you? Why now?
2. Can you state formidable founders + market + evidence in three sentences, all true?
3. Is your investor list ordered by expected value?
4. Who has made a definite offer, with no contingencies?
5. Using 1 / (1 − n): does this round improve your outcome by more than it costs?
