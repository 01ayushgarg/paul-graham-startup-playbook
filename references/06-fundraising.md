# 06 · Fundraising

> "Raising money is the second hardest part of starting a startup. The hardest part is making something
> people want." [fundraising, Aug 2008]

## Fundraising in one sentence

> "Avoid investors till you decide to raise money, and then when you do, talk to them all in parallel,
> prioritized by expected value, and accept offers greedily. That's fundraising in one sentence."
> [fr, Sep 2013]

## The rules [fr]

| Rule | In his words |
|---|---|
| When | "Don't raise money unless you want it and it wants you." |
| Mode | "A startup should either be in fundraising mode or not." |
| No till yes | "Treat investors as saying no till they unequivocally say yes, in the form of a definite offer with no contingencies." |
| Order | "Expected value = how likely an investor is to say yes, multiplied by how good it would be if they did." |
| Next steps | "Never leave a meeting with an investor without asking what happens next." |
| Close | "It's not a deal till the money's in the bank." |
| "We don't lead" | Translate as "No, except yes if you turn out to be a hot deal." |
| How much | "Multiply the number of people you want to hire times $15k times 18 months." |
| Valuation | "Valuation is at best third", after getting the money and good investors. |
| Accept | "If someone makes you an acceptable offer, take it." |
| Dilution | "Not to sell more than 25% in phase 2, on top of whatever you sold in phase 1, which should be less than 15%." |
| Stop | "Stop fundraising when you start to get a lot of air in the straw." |

`[2013]` The dollar figures are his 2013 numbers.

## Survival rules [fundraising]

- "Deals fall through... assume it will fall through."
- "Put the startup first. Fit meetings with investors into the spare moments in your development schedule."
- "If someone reputable offers you funding on reasonable terms, take it."
- "There is nothing investors like more than a startup that seems like it's going to succeed even without
  them."
- "The worst case scenario is the long no, the no that comes after months of meetings."

## How to convince investors [convince, Aug 2013]

> "You need three things: formidable founders, a promising market, and (usually) some evidence of success so
> far."

> "Formidable is roughly justifiably confident." "The way to seem most formidable as an inexperienced founder
> is to stick to the truth."

> "Founders think of startups as ideas, but investors think of them as markets." Find a "why now?" and "some
> plausible sequence of hops that leads to dominating a big market."

> "Make something worth investing in. Understand why it's worth investing in. Explain that clearly to
> investors."

> "Make the truth good, then just tell it."

From his posts: "If you make users love you, investors will love you too."
[X 2025-08-28](https://x.com/paulg/status/1961101566418833727) "Confused investors don't find your startup
intriguingly mysterious. They find it boring and lame." [X 2022-08-30](https://x.com/paulg/status/1564483027748548609)

## Herd dynamics [herd, Aug 2013]

> "The biggest component in most investors' opinion of you is the opinion of other investors."

> "So don't be demoralized by how hard it is to get the first commitment... The second will be easier."

> "Do not, however, tell A who B is."

## Is the trade worth it? The equity equation [equity, Jul 2007]

> "You should give up n% of your company if what you trade it for improves your average outcome enough that
> the (100 - n)% you have left is worth more than the whole company was before."

Break-even multiple = 1/(1 − n). Example from the essay: giving 7% is worth it if it improves the outcome
by more than 7.5%. "You should always feel richer after trading equity."

## Don't talk to corp dev [corpdev, Jan 2015]

> "Do you want to sell your company right now? Not eventually, right now. If not, just don't take the first
> meeting." The script: "Sorry, but we're focusing on growing the company."

**Checks to run:**
1. Do you want money, and does it want you? Why now?
2. Can you state formidable founders + market + evidence in three sentences, all true?
3. Is your investor list ordered by expected value?
4. Who has made a definite offer, with no contingencies?
5. Using 1/(1 − n): does this round improve your outcome by more than it costs?
