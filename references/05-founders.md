# 05 · Founders

## Relentlessly resourceful

> "A couple days ago I finally got being a good startup founder down to two words: relentlessly
> resourceful." [relres, Mar 2009]

Not merely relentless, because the problems are novel: "you don't know whether you're about to plow through
a block of foam or granite. So you have to be resourceful." [relres] The opposite is hapless: battered by
circumstances, letting the world have its way with you. [relres]

## Determination beats intelligence

> "We learned quickly that the most important predictor of success is determination. At first we thought
> it might be intelligence." [determination, Sep 2009]

His summary: "it consists of willfulness balanced with discipline, aimed by ambition." [determination] He
pictures will and discipline as two fingers squeezing a melon seed: both must press equally or the seed
spins off sideways. [determination]

## The five qualities YC looks for [founders, Oct 2010]

Paraphrased from the essay, with one short quote each where the wording matters:

1. **Determination.** Above a threshold of intelligence, it matters most. [founders]
2. **Flexibility.** Determination with the ability to change direction; his image is a running back.
   [founders]
3. **Imagination.** "In the startup world, most good ideas seem bad initially." [founders]
4. **Naughtiness.** Founders who "delight in breaking rules, but not rules that matter." He credits Sam
   Altman with the interview question: a time "they'd hacked something to their advantage." [founders]
5. **Friendship.** Startups pull cofounders apart if they can be pulled apart. [founders]

In a 2024 post he gave a shorter version: good at building, interested in the problem, very determined, and
talks a lot to users. [X 2024-11-25](https://x.com/paulg/status/1861029051722326339)

His 2005 test for good people: "Could you describe the person as an animal?" If you laugh at the sentence
"so-and-so is an animal", they're not. [start, Mar 2005]

## Persistent, not obstinate

> "The persistent are attached to the goal. The obstinate are attached to their ideas about how to reach
> it." [persistence, Jul 2024]

"Resilience means setbacks can't change your morale, not that they can't change your mind." [persistence]
A test for whether you're converging, from 2006: if each new idea reuses most of what you built for the
last, "you're probably in a process that converges." [startupmistakes, Oct 2006]

## Toughness

In 2026 he answered a question about the most underappreciated quality in founders by asking what's most
underappreciated about startups: how hard they are. "So the most underappreciated quality in founders is
sheer toughness." [X 2026-02-26](https://x.com/paulg/status/2026877766118105504)

## Cofounders

"Cofounders are for a startup what location is for real estate." [13sentences, Feb 2009] Looking back,
founders wished they'd paid more attention to "character and commitment, not ability." [really, Oct 2009]
On going solo: "Not having a cofounder is a real problem. A startup is too much for one person to bear."
[notnot, Mar 2007]

How to find them: "You find cofounders by working on projects with them." [google, Mar 2024] He says the
optimal startup has two or three founders. [google]

## Be good

*Be good* is part of his advice, not just a nicety. In *Be Good* he argues that being good helps startups
in three ways: "it improves their morale, it makes other people want to help them, and above all, it helps
them be decisive." [good, Apr 2008] The last one is the point: "the most important advantage of being good
is that it acts as a compass" when there are too many choices. [good] He adds that if you're benevolent,
people will rally around you: investors, customers, other companies and potential employees. [good]

Six years later, in *Mean People Fail*: "Startups don't win by attacking. They win by transcending." [mean,
Nov 2014]

## What it's really like

"Unconsciously, everyone expects a startup to be like a job, and that explains most of the surprises."
[really] It "will take over your life to a degree you cannot imagine." [before, Oct 2014] And expertise in
startups isn't the goal: be an expert "on your users and the problem you're solving for them." [before]
See chapter 13 for whether to start at all.

## How to apply (our suggestion)

Use these on yourself and on a prospective cofounder. The format is ours.

1. **The hack story.** Each founder tells one story of hacking a system to their advantage, without breaking
   rules that matter. No story is a yellow flag. [founders]
2. **Foam or granite.** Describe the last plan that failed and what you did next. Look for resourcefulness,
   not just persistence. [relres]
3. **Goal versus method.** Write the goal in one line, then list three ideas you hold about how to reach it.
   Which would you drop if the evidence said so? If none, that's obstinacy. [persistence]
4. **The project test for cofounders.** Have you shipped something together? If not, do a two-week project
   before splitting equity. [google]
5. **The compass test.** For a hard choice this month, ask which option is better for users. [good]

## Worked scenario (invented)

Two would-be cofounders met at a hackathon a month ago. One has a story of reverse-engineering a ticketing
site's API to find spare tickets; the other has none, but rebuilt a failing project twice. Neither has worked
with the other beyond the weekend.

**Our reading:** both show signs he looks for (naughtiness; resourcefulness after a failure). The gap is
friendship and knowing each other's work [founders, google]: run a project together before incorporating.

## Failure modes

- **Hiring a cofounder for a resume.** He points to character and commitment over ability. [really]
- **Obstinacy dressed as persistence.** [persistence]
- **Going solo by default.** [notnot, founders]
- **Mean as a strategy.** [mean, good]

**Checks to run on a founder (including yourself):**
1. Tell a story of a time you hacked something to your advantage.
2. Foam or granite: what did you do the last time a plan failed?
3. Which of your ideas are you attached to, versus the goal?
4. Would your cofounder relationship survive a year of bad news?
